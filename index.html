Flood Risk & Tax Base

See how sea level rise and inland flooding could affect property values and municipal tax revenue, parcel by parcel.

  1. Pick a town and select Analyze. The app gathers parcels, FEMA flood zones, lidar ground elevations and NOAA sea level rise projections. Most towns load in seconds.
  2. Choose a scenario and year to see the added annual tax revenue at risk compared with today, in today's dollars.
  3. Explore and export with the map, the sortable parcel ledger, a CSV, or a printable report.

This application is provided by the Southeastern Connecticut Council of Governments (SECOG) for general planning and screening purposes only.

Results are estimates built from public data (CT CAMA parcel data, FEMA flood maps, USGS lidar and NOAA projections) and simplified assumptions. They are not a flood insurance determination, an appraisal, an engineering study or a revenue forecast, and they do not reflect the official assessment of any property.

SECOG and its member municipalities make no warranties, express or implied, about the accuracy, completeness or suitability of this information for any particular purpose. Users assume full responsibility for any use of the information provided here.

    Method and sources

    Parcels and values. Parcel boundaries with assessed and appraised land and building values come live from the Connecticut CAMA and Parcel Layer. Connecticut assesses property at 70% of appraised value.

    Ground elevation. Each parcel is sampled at a point inside its boundary (five points in finer mode) against the USGS 3DEP lidar elevation service, in feet NAVD88. Elevations for each town's parcels are saved ahead of time and refreshed quarterly; only points missing from the saved set are requested live. Only parcels in or near a FEMA flood zone, or within reach of the coast, are sampled.

    Sea level rise. NOAA 2022 Interagency relative sea level scenarios load for the nearer of the Bridgeport and New London gauges, relative to 2005. Mean higher high water is interpolated between those gauges. Land below future high tide counts as permanently lost only if it is dry today.

    Coastal flooding. Parcels in FEMA VE or coastal AE zones use their base flood elevation; other parcels within reach of a VE zone use the town's coastal still-water elevation. Both rise one-for-one with sea level after 2020. This bathtub approach ignores wave attenuation and flood protection.

    Inland flooding. Parcels in riverine A, AE, AO and AH zones from the FEMA National Flood Hazard Layer use the base flood elevation (or zone depth) plus a rise in flood elevation that grows with the scenario. Parcels in the 0.2% annual chance zone begin to flood once that rise exceeds their assumed margin.

    Buildings. Each building in the CT 2D Building Footprints layer is placed on the parcel that contains it, and its ground elevation is sampled at the footprint. Flood depth and tidal loss for buildings use each building's own ground, with the parcel's building value split by footprint area. Land loss uses the parcel's ground points. Parcels with no mapped building fall back to the parcel point.

    Flood extent and depth. The map shading and the flooded-area chart compare USGS lidar ground elevation with the same water levels used for parcels: future high tide, the coastal 1% still-water elevation or the FEMA base flood elevation, each raised for the scenario and year. This bathtub method does not model flood barriers, drainage or wave effects.

    From depth to dollars. Building damage follows the USACE generic depth–damage curve for one-story homes without basements, applied to depth above the first floor. Tax at risk adds assessed value lost to permanent inundation, a market discount on newly exposed parcels, and the annualized reassessment after a design flood. All dollar figures are in today's dollars: assessments and the mill rate stay at current values, with no inflation or appreciation, so the cumulative figure uses a real discount rate.

    Use with care. This is a screening tool. A point inside each parcel stands in for the building location, elevations are bare-earth, and the assumptions panel holds values to replace with local figures. Connecticut's planning standard is 0.5 m (20 in) of sea level rise by 2050 (CGS 25-68o).
